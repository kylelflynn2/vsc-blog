01

Executive summary

The Chicago Blackhawks are 7-14-2, and captain Connor Bedard is expected to miss roughly three to four months after surgery on his right shoulder. Five years into the rebuild, the organization has built a talented young core, but it has a roster construction problem: too many young, offense-first players, too few who defend, win puck battles and play with physicality, and not enough veteran leadership around them. The analytics show it, with Chicago among the league’s worst in goals against and shot volume. Most of its young players are still under 23 years old. With Bedard out, I recommend treating the rest of 2026-27 as a development year. Chicago should avoid desperation moves, use the season to build the heavy forecheck Jeff Blashill has incorporated and let its young players learn it, sell the veterans who are not part of the long-term plan before the deadline, and protect its core: Bedard, Frondell, Kantserov, Byram, Levshunov, Knight and its own 2027 first-round pick, with Frank Nazar and Sam Rinzel kept unless a star becomes available.

The 2027 offseason is where the roster changes, and Chicago should use its surplus draft picks and prospects to address three needs. The first is top-six help. Because Bedard will have had two shoulder surgeries in five months, at 21, I would push for a two-way center so he can move to the wing permanently; if that center is not available, a power-forward winger who can support Bedard is the alternative. That addition is the move that tells the league Chicago is serious. The second is a stabilizing, defensive-minded veteran on the blue line who protects the front of the net. The third is more veterans who are high-motor and physical and bring the vocal leadership this team is lacking, along with an experienced backup goalie to share the load with Spencer Knight. Those veterans, on short-term deals, would surround the young core while prospects such as Vaclav Nestrasil, Ryan Roobroeck and Xavier Villeneuve develop. By the 2028 offseason, with the salary cap projected to rise to $127.5 million, Chicago will have the room to go after elite talent and complete the move from a rebuilding team to a serious playoff contender.

02

Introduction

I am a recent finance graduate pursuing a career in hockey operations, and a lifelong Blackhawks fan. This proposal lays out how Chicago can move from a rebuilding team to a playoff-caliber one. It starts with how the Blackhawks got here and where they stand now, with Connor Bedard out after shoulder surgery. From there, it explains why the rest of this season should be a development year and what the analytics say about the roster. It closes with the moves that can make Chicago a playoff team, starting in the 2027 offseason.

03

How we got here

The rebuild began about five years ago, when Kyle Davidson took over as interim general manager. He traded Alex DeBrincat, Brandon Hagel and Dominik Kubalik, accumulated high draft picks and built a pipeline of young talent. That phase worked. The next phase has stalled, and at its core this is a roster construction problem and a developmental problem.

The 2025-26 season started with promise. The team looked competitive out of the gate, and Bedard was the third-leading scorer in the NHL with 37 points in 25 games as of Dec. 2, according to The Hockey News. Since Bedard went down in December 2025, the team has never fully recovered.

In March of 2026, Chicago traded Connor Murphy (March 2), Jason Dickinson and Colton Dach (March 4) and Nick Foligno (March 6) within five days, a group Blashill called “great role models” for the young players, according to SI. The team’s record was 23-28-10 after the Dickinson trade, and it finished 29-39-14, which works out to 6-11-4 over its last 21 games without those veterans. I believe that stretch showed the front office what the young group would look like without veteran leadership around it. And that group was underwhelming. So why did we think things would magically change this season without replacing those veterans?

In the offseason, the Blackhawks signed Patrick Kane to add the veteran leadership this team lacked at the end of last season. Kane had the option to sign with the Buffalo Sabres, his hometown team, but decided to return to Chicago. Without him, Chicago would have entered this season with Anton Frondell and Roman Kantserov as its two big forward additions, even though Frondell played only 12 games at the end of last season. Connor Bedard, coach Jeff Blashill and the rest of the organization came into this season expecting to compete for a playoff spot. Kyle Davidson truly believed these young players, most with little NHL experience, were ready to take the next step without much veteran help beyond Kane.

I did not believe they were ready, and I was surprised by Davidson’s plan. The only evidence for it was the hot start in 2025-26, and that start did not last. Chicago is 7-14-2 through its first 23 games, and these young players have proven that they are talented but not quite ready to win without veteran help. Even Davidson has finally acknowledged it. “Could we have benefited from one more veteran? It’s very possible we may have,” he said, according to Chicago Hockey Now.

04

Where we are now

Then came another setback. On Nov. 21, the same day as a 4-3 overtime win in Calgary, the team announced that captain Connor Bedard will have surgery on his right shoulder, ESPN reported. He hurt it on a faceoff against Toronto on Nov. 17. Bedard first injured that shoulder on a faceoff against the St. Louis Blues last December and chose to rehab it instead of having surgery. That briefly worked. Then, during an offseason practice in his hometown of Vancouver, he hurt his left shoulder. He had surgery in July and missed roughly three months, including the first six games of this season. Through all of that, the right shoulder was never repaired, and that decision has come back to hurt him. Getting the surgery done now is the smart thing to do, but it most likely ends the Blackhawks’ playoff hopes this season.

Kyle Davidson said the return timeline “will be determined after surgery,” per Blackhawk Up. After the July procedure, Bedard came back in a little over three months, ahead of a four-month estimate, so a similar recovery would keep him out until at least March, CBS Sports noted, citing the Sun-Times’ Ben Pope. That points to an absence of roughly three to four months. Before the injury, Bedard had 7 goals and 11 assists for 18 points in 15 games.

There are clear positives. Sam Rinzel has looked great. From the eye test, Artyom Levshunov seems to have become a better player, and you are no longer holding your breath every time he touches the puck. “He’s not totally there yet, but he’s more comfortable in the systems, he’s more comfortable in his skin,” Jeff Blashill said, per the Sun-Times. However, when you look at his player card below, it tells a different story: a 1% projected WAR and 0% even-strength defense.

The clearest weakness is defense. Chicago allows 4.00 goals per game, 31st in the NHL, per StatMuse. The goal in reconstructing this roster should be to build a team where the young core develops alongside experienced veterans instead of carrying the team alone.

The team should be on the hunt for veteran defensemen who have proven it at the NHL level and fit the timeline, and I believe the front office is looking for exactly that. NHL reporter Frank Seravalli reported on Chicago Sports Network on Nov. 13 that Chicago is looking for a veteran defenseman “more along the lines of a Bowen Byram-type acquisition,” according to Chicago Hockey Now. However, the offensive numbers are not substantial either. At 5-on-5, Chicago is scoring 1.91 goals and generating 1.96 expected goals per 60 minutes, per HockeyStats. Seravalli also reported that the front office is looking for a “shoot-first scoring winger with size,” per the Sun-Times. That is easier said than done, because every GM in the league is looking for that player. Still, this is where the organization stands as of now.

They are still very young

It is easy to forget how young this group is. Frondell is 19. Bedard and Levshunov are 21. Nazar, Rinzel and Kantserov are 22. All six, along with Oliver Moore, are under 23. These are players who should be developing, not players who should be expected to carry an NHL team by themselves. The organization has to give them time to grow into the players they are expected to be.

05

The rest of this season is a development year

With Bedard out until at least March, I would not make moves to save this season. Chicago entered Saturday with the NHL’s second-worst record, per ESPN, and trading picks or prospects for a veteran now would spend assets without changing where this season ends. The rest of 2026-27 should go to development. The young players get the top minutes, the power-play time and the hard matchups, with the understanding that they will make mistakes and learn from them. This is the season for them to become their own players, learn the system and develop the right way. Patrick Kane said on Nov. 18 that “more guys will get more opportunity and a chance for a little more ice time,” per Blackhawk Up. That opportunity should go to Nazar, Kantserov, Frondell, Rinzel and Levshunov, and to prospects who earn a look, such as Hayes, Rockford’s leading scorer, and Lardis.

That is also why I would not chase a deal like the one Chicago explored for Rickard Rakell. The Blackhawks talked with Pittsburgh about a trade, but Rakell blocked it: Chicago is one of the eight teams on his no-trade list, TSN’s Pierre LeBrun reported. That move made sense while Bedard was playing and the organization was trying to compete for a playoff spot, because Rakell would have made the team better right away. Without Bedard, that is no longer the goal. Punting on this season and putting the focus on developing the young group might not be the worst thing in the world, and desperation moves are the last thing Chicago needs right now. Instead, the front office should spend the next few months building its list for the summer, when the big additions can be made.

06

Why Waiting Won’t Work

The plan comes together in the 2027 offseason. Chicago should always be looking to add, but with Bedard out, they can be more selective and in no rush to make a move. At that point, Chicago should protect the core and its own 2027 first-round pick at all cost, then use its other picks, prospects and players on their NHL roster, along with free agency, to add a two-way depth, veteran defensive help, and experience and physicality around this young group.

With that being said, I believe Davidson has become very attached to the players he has drafted, or he has not yet found trades that make sense against what he believes some of these young players will become, and he may be overvaluing their full potential. Waiting only pays off if they reach it, and that is not guaranteed. With the rebuild at a standstill, Chicago has to make logical moves, not desperation moves, that let the Blackhawks compete again starting next season.

This offseason is especially important because Chicago cannot keep losing beyond this season. This is the fifth year of the rebuild, and a young group that keeps losing will get comfortable with it, and losing will become part of its identity. The risk is the 2010s Edmonton Oilers, who missed the playoffs for ten straight seasons after 2006 and selected first overall four times in six years, according to The Hockey Writers. In a December 2014 Grantland piece, Sean McIndoe wrote that it is possible to install a losing culture, and that “an organization has lost so much for so long that it starts to have an effect on everyone who works there.” Even after the Oilers had added a generation of top picks, The Hockey News wrote in March 2019 that “there is something wrong with Edmonton’s culture and it has been wrong for a long time.” Draft capital alone does not end that cycle. Chicago needs results, traction and progress so that it can start playing winning hockey.

07

Assets and Priorities

Roster construction

I do not believe this roster, as built, is a Stanley Cup contender. Even if every young player reaches his full potential, the pieces overlap too much. Davidson has done a strong job building this organization, but there are specific additions that would make this a serious playoff team.

This is a roster construction problem. Talented young players are asked to carry the matchups and the minutes before they are capable of doing so. Chicago has too many of the same type, young, fast and offense-first, and too few who defend, win puck battles and play with physicality. On defense, Rinzel, Byram and Levshunov are offensive defensemen who skate well. Up front, Nazar, Moore, Bedard, Kantserov and Lardis are fast, high-motor goal scorers. That is a strong core, but a roster cannot consist only of that. The Sun-Times has noted that the team lacks size among its top-nine forwards, and NHL reporter Frank Seravalli reported that the front office wants a shoot-first scoring winger with size.

The numbers support that. The power play ranks near the bottom of the league, which suggests that skill alone is not enough without a net-front presence. As of Nov. 3, the team was 4-10-1, and only Vancouver had fewer points. Seravalli said on CHSN’s broadcast of a game against Utah: “They’re going to need to figure out how to create offense and win as they’re currently constituted,” The Rink reported.

Build the system this season

The first move costs nothing: use the rest of this season to build the right system. Blashill has already brought in an aggressive, puck-pressure forecheck, mostly a 2-1-2. “We want to be a puck-pursuit team,” he said, per the Sun-Times. It is the forecheck Carolina and Vegas run. Carolina sends two forecheckers in hard while its defensemen pinch down the walls to force turnovers, according to NHL.com’s Coaches’ Room, and the third forward (F3) trails the play to cover for the pinching defenseman. Vegas does it with a heavy, physical forecheck, as it showed in Game 1 of the Stanley Cup Final. Both run on size, a mobile back end and veterans who finish every check. It is a system that wins championships.

Before Bedard went down, Chicago needed to change this system, because it was in win-now mode without the size or the defensemen who can pinch and recover. It got pinned in its own end, and Blashill said the team had “allowed the most 2-on-1 rushes in the league by a lot,” per Yahoo Sports. With Bedard out, this season is the time to teach it, let the young players develop their games within it and go into next season a complete team.

What to do with Kevin Korchinski

Chicago also has to decide what to do with Kevin Korchinski. The 22-year-old has played five games this season, with one assist, per Hockey-Reference, and the coaching staff does not appear to trust him. Blashill has said he has “a habit of drifting back when he’s got the puck in the neutral zone,” per The Hockey News. Sitting in the press box does him no good, and Chicago cannot risk putting a former seventh overall pick on waivers and losing him for nothing. His name is already circulating, according to David Pagnotta, as NHL Trade Rumors relayed. He may be a change-of-scenery player, and a sweetener in a deal during the season or in the offseason.

The offseason is then the time to pursue veterans who will help the young group develop.

Players to avoid

Two names have been linked to Chicago as trade options: Ivan Miroshnichenko of the Capitals and Dmitri Voronkov of the Blue Jackets. I would avoid both.

Washington Capitals
Pass

That is too much for a 22-year-old who was drafted in the first round in 2022 and is still learning his game and his role. He has been productive in the AHL, and the Sun-Times says he skates well at 6-foot-1, 205 pounds. But he has dressed for only three games this season and has asked for a trade. The Sun-Times says his skating and work ethic “don’t get rave reviews,” and The Hockey News described him as a “poor man’s version” of Tyler Bertuzzi. A winger of this type does not solve a roster that needs both pace and size.

08

The 2027 Offseason

The 2027 offseason

This phase has to put Chicago in position to compete for a playoff spot and a deep run, and the order is simple: trade for a center first, add a stabilizing defenseman, then use free agency for physicality and veteran leadership.

Executing this plan will cost draft capital. Free agency is not what it used to be: teams increasingly trade a player, or his negotiating rights, ahead of July 1 rather than lose him for nothing. The Blackhawks should be prepared to pay in picks and prospects, and they have both. The Florida and Edmonton firsts are the primary assets and should be used aggressively as weapons, supplemented by the 2027 second- and third-round picks and, if necessary, the 2028 second and third.

The center option: Bedard moves to the wing

Davidson is prioritizing Connor Bedard, Anton Frondell and Frank Nazar as centers. My plan is to move Bedard to the wing permanently. He is about to have his second shoulder surgery in five months, at 21, and the right shoulder has now been hurt twice on faceoffs, last December and again on Nov. 17, according to ESPN. (The July surgery on his left shoulder followed an offseason training injury.) On the wing, he stops taking faceoffs and becomes a high-powered goal scorer.

The other half of the plan is trading for a two-way center who takes the load off Bedard: the faceoffs, the defensive-zone coverage, the toughest matchups and the hard-nosed, gritty work it takes to win, so Bedard can use his elite shot and focus on scoring. Early in 2025-26 he had 44 points, including 19 goals, in 31 games, according to The Hockey Writers, a pace of roughly 116 points over 82 games. That is the production I believe he can sustain as a winger next to a two-way center who can distribute the puck.

Quinton Byfield is the center I would pursue this offseason. Los Angeles is 7-12 and near the bottom of the Western Conference, has the highest average age in the NHL at 31.02, and looks like a team that could be heading toward a rebuild. Byfield is 24, 6-foot-5 and 230 pounds, per Hockey-Reference, and plays a two-way game. His Corsi this season is 62.9% (+15.2 relative) in 18 games, after 53.4% last season, when he had 24 goals and 49 points. He fits Chicago’s timeline, he does not have a no-movement clause, and his contract runs through 2028-29 at $6.25 million a year, according to PuckPedia. His HockeyStats card backs it up: an 84% projected WAR, 87% even-strength offense, 68% defense and 87th-percentile competition as a first-line center.

That contract matters. The cap is projected to rise to $113.5 million in 2027-28 and $127.5 million in 2028-29, Daily Faceoff reported, citing Elliotte Friedman. Byfield’s cap hit leaves Chicago room to pursue free agents in the summer of 2027, and the bigger jump in 2028 gives it room to go after elite talent right around the time prospects such as Vaclav Nestrasil, Ryan Roobroeck and Xavier Villeneuve, and even Sacha Boisvert and Marek Vanacker, could be ready, if they are not used as trade pieces first.

Elias Pettersson is the alternative, but he is harder to get. He is signed through 2032 at $11.6 million a year, and his full no-movement clause means he would have to approve any trade, according to Daily Faceoff. His card shows an 87% projected WAR and 95th-percentile competition, but his even-strength offense has fallen to the 41st percentile and his goals to the 30th, at $11.6 million for six more seasons.

The draft capital and the prospects are there to make a Byfield offer. Players and picks who could be moved in a deal like this include:

  • Marek Vanacker
  • Sacha Boisvert
  • Nick Lardis
  • Ethan Del Mastro
  • Kevin Korchinski
  • Florida’s 2027 first
  • Edmonton’s 2027 first
  • Vancouver’s 2027 second
  • Chicago’s 2028 second
  • Edmonton’s 2028 second

Some combination of those pieces will most likely be needed, and Oliver Moore could be the blue-chip piece Los Angeles asks for. If Chicago could acquire Byfield without giving up Moore, that would be ideal, because I truly believe in Moore’s upside. However, so do a lot of other teams.

The move also addresses the team’s clearest offensive problem, the power play. Blashill said in late October that “the power play is mirroring our 5-on-5 game: we can’t score,” per The Hockey News. A team in that position needs its most skilled shooters in the best positions, with centers who protect them. That is why the center comes first.

I also believe Frondell should move to the wing. The Sun-Times has argued that a move would not require abandoning Chicago’s long-term vision of him as a center and could be undone quickly if it does not work. I will not dive too far into this, because Chicago clearly wants to develop him as a center, and hopefully into the next Aleksander Barkov.

If Bedard stays at center: Owen Tippett

If Chicago cannot land a two-way center and keeps Bedard at center, a player who makes too much sense for them is Owen Tippett, a power-forward winger who can support Bedard. He is 6-foot-2, plays on a struggling Philadelphia team, and scored 28 goals and 51 points in 81 games last season, per ESPN. Tippett’s HockeyStats card shows a 79% projected WAR, 92nd-percentile goal scoring and an 86% penalties rating, with six years left at $6.2 million a year. The Flyers have received more trade calls on him than on Travis Konecny, and they are looking for a center with top-six upside, Daily Faceoff reported. Philadelphia and Chicago have already talked. David Pagnotta said on DFO Insider that “Philly’s checked in” on Moore and that there has been at least one conversation about him. Chicago could build an offer around Moore and one of the Edmonton or Florida first-round picks. If Philadelphia asks for Nazar, I would pass.

Other trade targets

Zach Whitecloud is a defenseman I would look to pursue. He brings stability to the defense corps and veteran leadership. He is 29, has a Stanley Cup ring and carries a $2.75 million cap hit, per Spotrac, and Daily Faceoff reported on Oct. 7 that other teams have interest. His card shows a 70% projected WAR with 83% even-strength defense and a 72% penalty-kill rating, though his WAR has slipped this season, with two years left at $2.8 million. I would not move a first-round pick for him, though. If that is the ask, I would pass and turn to free agency for a veteran defenseman instead.

Chicago should also look to pursue Detroit Red Wings defenseman Simon Edvinsson. He is an unsigned restricted free agent who must sign by Dec. 1 to be eligible to play this season, according to The Hockey News. If he does not sign, his situation carries into the offseason, and his price could be at its lowest then. If he is not traded and Detroit brings him back on a short deal for the rest of the season, similar to the one-year contract Carolina gave Alexander Nikishin, he would be a restricted free agent again next summer, and Chicago should pursue him in the 2027 offseason. TSN reported that up to 10 teams have been in talks and that the cost would resemble the deals for Noah Dobson, Mikhail Sergachev and Bowen Byram: multiple assets, including prospects and first-round picks. His stats show why he is a long-term piece more than an immediate stabilizer: a 73% even-strength offensive player with a 45% projected WAR, but an undesirable 22% even-strength defensive rating. If Chicago can acquire a player like him for prospects and draft capital alone, I would pursue it this summer. If Detroit asks for a blue-chip player such as Frank Nazar or Anton Frondell, I would pass.

Alexander Nikishin intrigues me as well, but the price is the problem. He signed a one-year, $3 million contract on Oct. 25 after requesting a trade, according to TSN, so he will be a restricted free agent again next summer. Before that deal, he asked for five years at $9 million a year, Elliotte Friedman reported. Carolina has also told teams that any Nikishin deal must include Jesperi Kotkaniemi, per David Pagnotta, which means taking on Kotkaniemi’s $4.82 million cap hit for four more years. Nikishin’s stats show the risk: a 21% projected WAR and 8% even-strength defense despite 97% finishing.

Free agency: experience and physicality

Chicago is set to lose Jordan Greenway, Teuvo Teräväinen and Andrew Mangiapane, who are all pending unrestricted free agents, and I do not expect to bring them back. Free agency is where the team becomes more experienced and adds physicality.

This is where the analytics come in. My free-agent list starts with Corsi, which measures the share of shot attempts a team takes while a player is on the ice. A Corsi For percentage (CF%) above 50 means his team is controlling play when he is out there, and CF% rel compares that with his team’s results when he is on the bench. Chicago cannot hold the puck, so I want players who help it do that, or who bring size and physicality that the numbers do not capture.

The numbers split the list in two. McCann drives play better than anyone here, and his CF% rel is elite. His 5-on-5 share is closer to even (49.8% this season) but still +10.3 relative to his team. The concern is health. He missed a 17-game stretch, a seven-game stretch and the last three games of 2025-26, according to The Hockey Writers, and an October report listed on The Stanley Cap said he would miss about 10 weeks with a lower-body injury. Lehkonen would be an amazing fit for this group and would give the top line real offensive firepower, but he wants to stay in Colorado: Chris Johnston said he “would love to remain” there, NHL Rumors reported. Colorado may not be able to keep him, though. Cale Makar’s record $20.4 million cap hit starts next season, according to TSN, and Mile High Sticking estimates the Avalanche have about $12.5 million in space for 2027-28. Even if Colorado ends up trading his rights, he will most likely want to play for a contender, and Colorado will most likely want NHL-caliber players back now, not prospects and picks. Carlo, Coleman, Gallagher and Anderson are not play drivers by Corsi. Their value is size, physicality and experience.

The three players I’d pursue:

  • Ryan Hartman or Blake Coleman, for physicality and leadership. Hartman, whom Chicago drafted 30th overall in 2013, per Hockey-Reference, is a right shot who plays wing and center, brings a vocal presence and would be a third- or fourth-liner. He plays with an edge but stays disciplined: his card has a 78th-percentile penalties rating, which means he draws more penalties than he takes. Coleman plays a hard, defensive game, has won the Stanley Cup twice with Tampa Bay and, at 35, fits a one-year deal. His card shows he brings more than grit, with 85% even-strength offense and a 62% penalty-kill rating against 78th-percentile competition.
  • Jared McCann, for top-six offensive upside. His card shows 82nd-percentile finishing, a 79% power-play rating and a 98% penalties rating, which would help a power play that ranks 27th. His even-strength defense (46%) and his health are the questions.
  • Brandon Carlo, as a right-shot veteran defenseman. He is 6-foot-5, physical and good in front of his own net, and he would bring veteran leadership on the right side. St. Louis acquired him in June for his size, length, experience and penalty killing, NHL.com reported, and his card fits that role exactly: 83% even-strength defense and an 88% penalty-kill rating, with a 52% projected WAR that has climbed this season. His card also shows 18% even-strength offense and 5% first assists, so he would be a defensive defenseman, not a puck mover.

The contract structure matters as much as the names. The young players need to develop year by year, and Chicago should not block that development with long contracts for aging veterans. McCann, 30, and Carlo, 29, are young enough to be part of this team’s future, so four- or five-year deals make sense for them. Hartman, 32, fits a three-year deal, and his age is part of what makes him more intriguing than Coleman: he can be a veteran presence for this group for longer. If Chicago pursues Coleman, 35, Gallagher or Anderson, those should be one- or two-year deals.

A backup goalie would also be a big help. Spencer Knight has started 18 of Chicago’s 23 games. I understand Chicago recalled Drew Commesso to be Knight’s full-time backup, after placing Arvid Soderblom on waivers and later trading him to the New York Rangers, but he has not proven he is ready for that role. His numbers in Rockford were mediocre (2.74 goals-against average and .890 save percentage in seven games, per StatsCrew). A veteran who could play 25 to 30 games would ease Knight’s workload and give Commesso time to develop in Rockford until he tears up the AHL. Two pending free agents fit. Anton Forsberg, 33, has a .901 save percentage and 2.60 goals-against average for Los Angeles this season despite an 0-7-1 record, after a .910 in 36 games last season, per Hockey-Reference. Philipp Grubauer, 34, has an .894 and 3.02 for Seattle, after a .909 in 32 games. My estimate is that Forsberg’s next deal lands at one or two years and $1.5 million to $2.25 million a year, and Grubauer’s at one or two years and $2 million to $3 million.

Their cards make the choice easier. Forsberg has a 36% projected WAR, with 57% even-strength play and 91% excellent starts, while Grubauer sits at 17%, with 23% even-strength play, 10% quality starts and 1% on low-danger shots.

(You can find both goalies’ cards in the Player Cards tab.)

No single signing fixes this team alone. Together, these players give Chicago what it is lacking: scoring, physicality, vocal leadership and high-motor veterans who can play aggressively alongside its developing young players. Jared McCann or Artturi Lehkonen could step into the top six immediately and bring that scoring. Players such as Blake Coleman, Ryan Hartman, Brandon Carlo, Brendan Gallagher and Josh Anderson bring the vocal leadership and the aggression to win puck battles and do the little things the young players are still learning to do.

Will players want to come to Chicago?

The question at the end of all of this is whether players will want to come to Chicago. It is a tough sell for a bottom-five team in the NHL, and Jason Robertson shows why. His card is the best in this proposal: a 99% projected WAR, with 96% even-strength offense, power play and goals. The 27-year-old led Dallas with 45 goals and 96 points last season and is playing on a one-year deal, and the Stars want a long-term extension before July, TSN reported. If that does not happen, he is the most likely star to reach unrestricted free agency or to have his rights dealt to a team of his choosing. Chicago inquired about him in June, Pierre LeBrun reported, per The Hockey News, but right now Chicago does not look like a destination for a player like that.

That is why Chicago needs to make one move that tells the rest of the league it is serious, and a trade for a center like Byfield or Pettersson is that move. Davidson has already said Chicago is “intent on improving the playing roster,” per Daily Faceoff. The first big move this offseason has to back that up, and once it does, the next free agent’s decision gets easier.

The 2027 draft

Unfortunately, this means that it is also time to talk about the 2027 draft prospects. Chicago entered Nov. 21 with the NHL’s second-worst record, per ESPN, which right now means the second-best odds to win the draft lottery, and with Bedard out, that probably will not change. In recent drafts, the second-worst team has had a 13.5% chance at the first pick, according to NBC Chicago.

Landon DuPont is the runaway favorite to go first overall. Both Daily Faceoff and TSN rank the right-shot defenseman first. He had 18 goals and 73 points in 63 games for Everett in the WHL last season and has 15 assists in 12 games at Michigan this season, per Elite Prospects. If Chicago wins the lottery, you pick DuPont and staple a future Quinn Hughes on that right side.

The second pick is more of a toss-up between Sergei Skvortsov, Nazar Privalov, Dima Zhilkin and Jonah Neuenschwander. Daily Faceoff ranked Skvortsov second, Privalov third and Zhilkin fourth in October, with Neuenschwander seventh; TSN had Privalov second, Skvortsov fourth and Neuenschwander ninth in September. If Chicago ends up with the second pick, my first choice is Skvortsov. He is the top-ranked winger on Daily Faceoff’s list, which praises his speed, his shot and his ability to get open against professionals, and I believe his talent and skill set will make him a star in this league. He had 19 goals and 43 points in 52 MHL games last season, and this season he has 10 points in nine MHL games, two points in 11 KHL games with Torpedo Nizhny Novgorod and seven points in three games for Russia’s under-20 team, per Elite Prospects.

My second choice is Neuenschwander. He is already in his third season of professional hockey: he debuted in Switzerland’s National League with EHC Biel-Bienne in November 2024 at 15, and became the youngest player in league history to record a point in his debut, NHL.com reported. He has eight points in 21 NL games this season, per Tankathon. His skating stands out. “His skating is excellent for such a big forward,” said Jukka-Pekka Vuorinen, NHL Central Scouting’s director of European scouting, and Elite Prospects wrote that his speed and skating “already match if not surpass many of the older players around him.” At 6-foot-3, with that experience against men already, he has second-line center written all over him.

Tankathon’s current mock draft has Chicago picking second and taking Privalov, with Skvortsov going third to Pittsburgh, Neuenschwander fourth and Zhilkin seventh. It also shows the difference in size: Privalov is 6-foot-4 and 227 pounds, Skvortsov 6-foot and 163.

I would still take Skvortsov. It may take him a few years to develop into an NHL-caliber player, since he is still playing mostly in the MHL. He is already playing against professionals in the KHL, but he has to show he can make that jump permanently. The skill, though, is undeniable. If Chicago could add him to this group, he would not only replace the kind of player it passed on in Ivan Demidov when it selected Levshunov second overall in 2024, but he would most likely give the Blackhawks the best prospect pool in the NHL.

09

Conclusion

Chicago does not need to abandon its rebuild. At 7-14-2, with Bedard out until at least March and almost no chance at the playoffs, the rest of this season belongs to the young players, and to their development into the players we expect them to become.

The 2027 offseason is where the roster transforms. The biggest move is trading for a two-way center so Bedard can move to the wing for good. After two shoulder surgeries in five months, he should stop taking faceoffs and focus on what he does best, which is scoring. That trade alone tells the league Chicago is serious. From there, Chicago adds a veteran defenseman who protects the net and stabilizes a young, offense-first blue line on a team allowing 4.00 goals per game, 31st in the NHL. Up front, it adds a top-six scorer who drives play and takes pressure off Bedard, which would also help a power play that ranks 27th at 18.5%, along with physical, vocal veterans who win puck battles and hold the young players accountable. These are much-needed moves to help this team compete for the playoffs next season, all while Chicago protects its core and its 2027 first-round pick.

While those veterans and the young core start winning together, the next wave keeps developing behind them. By 2028, prospects like Nestrasil, Roobroeck and Villeneuve will be ready to step into a winning team instead of carrying a losing one, and with more cap space, Chicago can go after even more elite talent to push it over the top. Chicago needs to develop now, upgrade this summer and start winning next season. That is how Chicago gets back to contending for the Stanley Cup.

Finally, the Blackhawks should schedule Jonathan Toews’ long-overdue jersey retirement ceremony, which should have happened already.

How to read the cards

The player cards come from HockeyStats.com, built by JFresh Hockey. Every number is a percentile: it shows how a player ranks against others at the same position, so 90% means he’s better than 90% of the league’s forwards (or defensemen, or goalies). Box colors follow the same scale, with blue for strong and red for weak. The numbers use three years of data weighted toward the most recent seasons, which is why a young player’s card can lag behind how he looks this year.

What the analytics say

As of right now, the underlying numbers point to the same problem the eye test does: Chicago cannot keep the puck out of its own net, and it does not generate enough shots to make up for it. Here is what the data shows, with sources.

Defense is the biggest gap

Over the last 21 games of 2025-26 and the first two of this season, Chicago produced 40.1 expected goals for and allowed 66.9, an expected-goals share of 37.9%, the worst in the NHL over that stretch, the Sun-Times reported using Natural Stat Trick. The team was outscored 66-43 at 5-on-5 in those games. For comparison, the share was 43.8% over the first 61 games of 2025-26, so the drop followed the March trades. This season, Chicago is allowing 4.00 goals per game, 31st in the NHL through Nov. 21, per StatMuse.

The same Sun-Times piece used HockeyStats defensive percentiles to show what left. Jason Dickinson graded in the 97th, 95th and 99th percentiles over the last three seasons, and Connor Murphy in the 85th, 85th and 97th. Only four other Hawks on this season’s roster (Oliver Moore, Teuvo Teräväinen, Jordan Greenway and Alex Vlasic) graded above average defensively last season. That is the problem in one sentence: the defensive responsibility fell on a group that had not shown it could carry it.

Shot volume and the power play

NHL EDGE shows that since 2020-21, Chicago ranks last in the league in both goals per game (2.55) and shots on goal per game (26.6), per NHL.com’s 32-in-32 EDGE preview. Chicago averaged 21.4 shots per game as of Oct. 24, second-lowest in the league, the Sun-Times reported. The same EDGE report has Chicago’s high-danger shooting percentage at 22.7% last season, tied with Buffalo for the best in the league. Chicago finishes the good chances it gets; it simply does not get enough of them. That points to a volume and structure problem, not a talent problem, and it supports adding a shoot-first winger with size.

The power play fits the same pattern. It ranked 29th last season at 16.9% per the EDGE report. It has improved since early November, when it sat 31st at 10.6%, according to The Hockey News, but it is still 27th at 18.5% through Nov. 21, per StatMuse.

The goalie is not the problem

EDGE has Spencer Knight with 432 high-danger saves last season, second in the NHL behind Ilya Sorokin’s 452, a .847 high-danger save percentage (sixth) and a .931 save percentage in 5-on-5 close situations (tied for third). He is facing a very high volume of dangerous chances and stopping them at an elite rate. Improving the defense in front of him is the most direct way to improve the team.

The team card does show Chicago’s goaltending below expected this season (−0.31), so Knight has not been perfect either. But the larger problem is still the volume of chances in front of him.

Baseball may soon come to a halt. The collective bargaining agreement between MLB and the MLB Players Association expires on Dec. 1 at 11:59 p.m. ET, and the league is widely expected to lock out the players as soon as it does. A lockout would freeze all transactions, including free agent signings and trades. Any move a team wants to make this winter has to happen before then.

The Milwaukee Brewers enter this offseason coming off the first World Series title in franchise history, but the championship roster is already changing. Several players will not be brought back, and Jake Bauers and Dustin May have already signed with new teams.

With the deadline approaching, this report answers five questions:

The $142 million budget is based on 2026 spending. Front Office Sports reported the Brewers' payroll at $141.9 million in September, 20th in MLB. This assumes the organization holds payroll roughly flat. If ownership spends more after a championship season, the plan only becomes easier to execute.

How I valued players

I used four measures to evaluate every option.

1. WAR from FanGraphs. All WAR figures in this report, both 2026 results and 2027 projections, are FanGraphs WAR (fWAR). For pitchers, FanGraphs calculates WAR from FIP rather than runs allowed, which reduces the influence of defense and luck.

2. Prices from ESPN and Spotrac. For contract costs, I used ESPN's contract projections. When ESPN did not project a player, I used Spotrac's market value. ESPN was the primary source because Spotrac's figures were often unrealistic. When neither source listed a player, I estimated a price from his 2026 salary and labeled it as an estimate.

3. The price of a win. Teams pay more per win for better players because high-end production is scarcer. Based on last winter's signings, FanGraphs identified three price levels:

Price per winFanGraphs
Projected WARPrice per win
Under 1 WAR$7.37M
1 to 2 WAR$8.60M
2+ WAR$11.76M

Multiplying a player's projected WAR by his price per win gives the market value of that production. Subtracting his actual salary gives his surplus. A positive surplus means the team is getting more value than it is paying for.

4. ERA minus FIP. FIP measures only what a pitcher controls: strikeouts, walks and home runs. When a pitcher's ERA is well above his FIP, he likely pitched through bad luck or poor defense and may be a buy-low candidate. Milwaukee has consistently targeted these pitchers and gotten more out of them.

Working through the 2027 free agents

I began by reviewing every available 2027 free agent. The Brewers lost significant contributors from their World Series roster, and the goal is to replace them without overspending.

Last season the Brewers spent approximately $142 million. With Bauers, May, Woodruff and the rest of the 2027 free agents off the books, the team has roughly $22 million to spend.

First base: This was the starting point. Jake Bauers outperformed his contract last season, and his production will be very difficult to replace. He hit .266/.389/.484 with 26 home runs, a 143 wRC+ and 3.6 WAR.

Nathaniel Lowe was the first option I considered. However, he projects to cost roughly $8 million, more than a third of the available budget, and FanGraphs projects him for only 0.8 WAR in 2027. I concluded that no first baseman in free agency could replace Bauers' production at a reasonable price.

That leaves Andrew Vaughn as the only established first baseman on the roster, and he was used primarily in a platoon role last season. The Brewers have two strong first base prospects in Blake Burke and Andrew Fischer, but neither has played in the majors. Burke was named Southern League MVP after hitting .285/.367/.603 with 30 home runs at Double-A Biloxi, then hit .236 in his first 14 games after a promotion to Triple-A Nashville. Fischer hit 46 home runs between High-A and Double-A. FanGraphs projects them for only 70 and 91 plate appearances in 2027, so neither can be counted on to replace Bauers yet.

One player worth pursuing on a minor-league deal is LaMonte Wade Jr. As a left-handed hitter, Wade could handle first base against right-handed pitching. This is a buy-low opportunity: if he has a breakout season similar to Bauers', it is a significant plus, and if he does not, the cost is minimal because he is on a minor-league deal. The plan would be to sign him and have him compete with Burke for a roster spot in spring training. I also reviewed the trade market for a left-handed first baseman; those options are at the end of this report.

Starting pitching: This looked like the biggest need, with at least one rotation spot open. I started by looking at David Peterson and Kris Bubic. Bubic posted 1.1 WAR in limited time, but ESPN projects him as a reliever, and his injury history is a concern. He had Tommy John surgery in 2023 and missed the second half of 2025 with a rotator cuff strain. In 2026 he went on the IL in May with elbow soreness, then suffered a shoulder setback during his rehab that moved him to the 60-day IL. As a result, he is not the best fit.

Erick Fedde also caught my eye, but his 3.83 ERA sat well below his 5.01 FIP, the luckiest ERA of any pitcher I evaluated. That makes him a strong risk for a regression, so I elected to pass on pursuing him.

I also evaluated Zac Gallen. Milwaukee has an outstanding track record of developing pitchers, and Spotrac valued him at about $7 million, so pairing Peterson and Gallen for a combined $13 to $14 million was appealing. However, ESPN projects Gallen at $12 million, and his 5.34 FIP was the worst of any pitcher I evaluated, so he stays on the list only as a buy-low option.

The internal options changed the question. Misiorowski, Henderson, Gasser and Harrison give Milwaukee four starters, with Shane Drohan and Chad Patrick competing for the fifth spot. Drohan threw 124.2 innings with a 3.73 FIP in 2026, and Patrick posted a 3.40 ERA over 106 innings. If that group holds up, the Brewers do not need a starter. They can add multiple relievers instead and stay well under budget.

However, if Harrison does not return to form, or Drohan and Patrick are not the answer for the fifth spot, Milwaukee will need a starter. David Peterson is the best value, and Michael Soroka is the backup plan if Peterson signs elsewhere.

Bullpen: With Romero and Senzatela gone, the Brewers need bullpen help on both sides. On the right side, Kevin Ginkel is the best fit. His 3.50 FIP was well below his 4.12 ERA, another buy-low scenario, and he projects for 0.6 WAR, the same as Jonathan Loáisiga, at roughly a third of the price. On the left side, Ashby and Hall return, with Drohan joining them if he does not win the fifth starter spot, and Tyler Alexander is a cost-effective replacement for Romero.

The rest of the roster: For backup catcher, I wanted to get younger, so Bo Naylor takes the role rather than spending on a veteran. The Brewers also just acquired Ryan Walker, who covers Senzatela's innings in the bullpen.

That led to two options if Milwaukee decides it needs a starter.

Question 3

Who do we sign?

If the current rotation holds up, the Brewers can skip the starter market and sign multiple relievers. Signing Kevin Ginkel and Tyler Alexander, for example, would cost about $5.5 million combined and leave payroll roughly $17 million under budget.

However, if Harrison is not the guy, or Drohan and Patrick are not the answer for the fifth spot, these are the players the Brewers should go after. Both options add one starter and one reliever, with Michael Soroka as the backup plan if Peterson signs elsewhere.

David Peterson is the best value on the board. His 4.79 ERA is misleading; his FIP was 3.96, nearly a full run better, which fits the profile Milwaukee has targeted successfully. FanGraphs projects him for 2.0 WAR in 2027, worth about $23.5 million on the open market, and ESPN prices him at $11 million a year. That is $12.5 million of surplus value.

Tyler Alexander is a cost-effective left-hander to replace Romero. His FIP (3.10) was better than his ERA (3.70), and $2 million is a low-risk price.

The other three moves add nothing to payroll. To get younger at backup catcher, Bo Naylor takes over Sánchez's role. Ryan Walker is already on the roster and covers Senzatela's innings. At first base, LaMonte Wade Jr. on a minor-league deal gives Andrew Vaughn a left-handed platoon partner, with Blake Burke developing behind them.

Kevin Ginkel replaces Alexander in this option, filling the right-handed setup role. He threw 67.2 innings in 2026 with a 3.50 FIP and a 26.2% strikeout rate, and projects for 0.6 WAR with a 3.64 FIP. Neither ESPN nor Spotrac lists a price for him, so I estimated $3.5 million based on his $2.7 million salary in 2026. Peterson, Naylor, Walker and Wade stay the same.

Michael Soroka is the backup plan in both options. If Peterson signs elsewhere, Soroka steps into his spot for only $1 million more, and he is still worth a look as a second starter if the rotation falls short. He threw 116 innings for the Diamondbacks in 2026 with a 3.41 ERA, 3.19 FIP and 2.7 WAR, and FanGraphs projects him for 1.8 WAR in 2027, worth about $15.5 million. At $12 million a year on a two-year deal, that is $3.5 million of surplus value, and adding him on top of either option would put payroll only $2.4 to $3.9 million over budget.

Trevor Megill is one player I would keep rather than trade. The Brewers are reportedly listening to offers for their closer, who is entering his final year of arbitration with a projected $8 million salary. However, Megill was the second most valuable pitcher on the staff in 2026, behind only Misiorowski, posting a 2.63 ERA, 1.85 FIP and 2.5 WAR over 61.2 innings. With Romero and Senzatela already gone, trading him would open another hole in a bullpen this plan is trying to fill. Keeping Megill alongside Uribe gives Milwaukee two proven late-inning arms, and his salary is already part of the arbitration estimate in the payroll above, so keeping him does not change the budget.

Both options leave the Brewers under budget, Option A by $9.6 million and Option B by $8.1 million. That leaves flexibility to add Michael Soroka or another reliever if the rotation is not producing.

The remaining gap of 5.1 to 5.5 wins is larger because both options rely on the internal rotation, not free agents, to cover May's and Woodruff's innings. As a group, those five project for 1.6 fewer wins than they produced in 2026. Across the entire roster, FanGraphs projects the current Brewers for 44.4 WAR in 2027, down from 48.7 in 2026. Still, this is the strongest reason to keep Michael Soroka in mind as a second starter if the rotation starts slowly.

Question 5

Do they fit what we're trying to do?

Every move is consistent with how Milwaukee builds its roster.

  • Short-term commitments. Every deal is two years or less: Peterson, Alexander, Ginkel and Soroka for two. With a new CBA coming and the rules uncertain, this avoids locking in long-term money.
  • Buy low on pitching. All three free agents posted a FIP better than their ERA (Peterson by 0.83, Ginkel by 0.62, Alexander by 0.60). The Brewers have built their success on acquiring these pitchers at a discount and improving them.
  • Get younger. Using Naylor at catcher and Burke at first base, rather than paying for veterans, keeps opportunities open for the young players expected to close the remaining gap.
  • Payroll discipline. Both options keep payroll around $132 to $134 million, $8 to $10 million under budget.
  • Balance. Peterson is left-handed, Option A adds a lefty in Romero's role, and Option B adds the right-handed setup arm the bullpen needs. Wade gives the right-handed Vaughn a left-handed platoon partner.
  • Completed before Dec. 1. None of these are headline signings that take all winter. All of them can be completed before a lockout freezes the market.

Shane McClanahan is a left-hander with a 3.50 FIP at about $6.6 million. With only one year of control remaining, the acquisition cost should be reasonable. His forearm would require a medical review, but acquiring arms below market value is the Brewers' formula.

Conclusion

If Misiorowski, Henderson, Gasser, Harrison and either Drohan or Patrick hold up, the Brewers do not need a starter and can add relievers while staying well under budget. If they need one, Option A signs David Peterson and Tyler Alexander for $13 million and 2.3 projected wins, and Option B signs David Peterson and Kevin Ginkel for $14.5 million and 2.7 projected wins, with Michael Soroka as the backup plan. In both, Naylor takes over at backup catcher, Walker covers the bullpen innings, and Wade competes with Burke at first base on a minor-league deal. If the starters are not producing, Soroka could be added alongside Peterson, and keeping Trevor Megill holds the back of the bullpen together.

Neither option recovers every lost win; no plan at this budget could. The rest relies on what Milwaukee does better than almost any organization, which is getting more out of its players than the projections expect.

However, timing matters. If the league locks out the players on Dec. 1, no transactions can be made until a new agreement is reached. None of these moves require a long negotiation, and the Brewers can complete all of them now.

Baseball may soon come to a halt. The collective bargaining agreement between MLB and the MLB Players Association expires on Dec. 1 at 11:59 p.m. ET, and the league is widely expected to lock out the players as soon as it does. A lockout would freeze all transactions, including free agent signings and trades. Any move a team wants to make this winter has to happen before then.

The Milwaukee Brewers enter this offseason coming off the first World Series title in franchise history, but the championship roster is already changing. Several players will not be brought back, and Jake Bauers and Dustin May have already signed with new teams.

With the deadline approaching, this report answers five questions:

The $142 million budget is based on 2026 spending. Front Office Sports reported the Brewers' payroll at $141.9 million in September, 20th in MLB. This assumes the organization holds payroll roughly flat. If ownership spends more after a championship season, the plan only becomes easier to execute.

How I valued players

I used four measures to evaluate every option.

1. WAR from FanGraphs. All WAR figures in this report, both 2026 results and 2027 projections, are FanGraphs WAR (fWAR). For pitchers, FanGraphs calculates WAR from FIP rather than runs allowed, which reduces the influence of defense and luck.

2. Prices from FanGraphs. For contract costs, I used the median AAV from FanGraphs' free agent crowdsource, where fans estimate each player's contract. It is a crowd estimate rather than an analyst's projection, but it reflects a broad view of the market. When the crowdsource did not list a player, I used ESPN's contract projections, then Spotrac's market value. When none of those listed a player, I estimated a price from his 2026 salary and labeled it as an estimate.

3. The price of a win. Teams pay more per win for better players because high-end production is scarcer. Based on last winter's signings, FanGraphs identified three price levels:

Price per winFanGraphs
Projected WARPrice per win
Under 1 WAR$7.37M
1 to 2 WAR$8.60M
2+ WAR$11.76M

Multiplying a player's projected WAR by his price per win gives the market value of that production. Subtracting his actual salary gives his surplus. A positive surplus means the team is getting more value than it is paying for.

4. ERA minus FIP. FIP measures only what a pitcher controls: strikeouts, walks and home runs. When a pitcher's ERA is well above his FIP, he likely pitched through bad luck or poor defense and may be a buy-low candidate. Milwaukee has consistently targeted these pitchers and gotten more out of them.

Working through the 2027 free agents

I began by reviewing every available 2027 free agent. The Brewers lost significant contributors from their World Series roster, and the goal is to replace them without overspending.

Last season the Brewers spent approximately $142 million. With Bauers, May, Woodruff and the rest of the 2027 free agents off the books, the team has roughly $22 million to spend.

First base: This was the starting point. Jake Bauers outperformed his contract last season, and his production will be very difficult to replace. He hit .266/.389/.484 with 26 home runs, a 143 wRC+ and 3.6 WAR.

Nathaniel Lowe was the first option I considered. However, he projects to cost roughly $8 million, more than a third of the available budget, and FanGraphs projects him for only 0.8 WAR in 2027. I concluded that no first baseman in free agency could replace Bauers' production at a reasonable price within the $142 million budget.

That leaves Andrew Vaughn as the only established first baseman on the roster, and he was used primarily in a platoon role last season. The Brewers have two strong first base prospects in Blake Burke and Andrew Fischer, but neither has played in the majors. Also, Fischer is primarily a third baseman. Burke was named Southern League MVP after hitting .285/.367/.603 with 30 home runs at Double-A Biloxi; however, he then hit .193 in his 31 games after a promotion to Triple-A Nashville. Fischer hit 46 home runs between High-A and Double-A. FanGraphs projects them for only 70 and 91 plate appearances for the Brewers in 2027, so neither can be counted on to replace Bauers yet.

One player worth pursuing on a minor-league deal is LaMonte Wade Jr. As a left-handed hitter, Wade could handle first base against right-handed pitching. This is a buy-low opportunity: if he has a breakout season similar to Bauers', it is a significant plus, and if he does not, the cost is minimal because he is on a minor-league deal. The plan would be to sign him and have him compete with Burke for a roster spot in spring training. I also reviewed the trade market for a left-handed first baseman; those options are at the end of this report.

Ty France is the guy if management is willing to spend. He hit .295/.363/.502 with 21 home runs, a 141 wRC+ and 3.6 WAR for the Padres in 2026, nearly matching Bauers' 143 wRC+ and 3.6 WAR, and the crowdsource prices him at $12 million a year over two years. FanGraphs projects some regression, to a 107 wRC+ and 1.4 WAR in 2027, but he would give Milwaukee an everyday first baseman. With the Brewers' DH spot belonging to Christian Yelich, Vaughn would move into a part-time role, starting at first base or DH against left-handed pitching. Signing France brings the offseason total to $33.5 million, and upgrading to him could also make Vaughn a trade candidate.

Starting pitching: This looked like the biggest need, with at least one rotation spot open. With May and Woodruff gone, adding one more starter would provide stability if Harrison, Drohan, Gasser, or Patrick does not perform up to expectations. I started by looking at Michael Soroka and David Peterson. Soroka was the stronger pitcher in 2026, with a 3.41 ERA, 3.19 FIP and 2.7 WAR over 116 innings, compared with Peterson's 4.79 ERA, 3.96 FIP and 1.7 WAR. Peterson is the cheaper option, at $11 million a year to Soroka's $18 million, and FanGraphs projects him slightly higher for 2027, at 2.0 WAR to Soroka's 1.8. However, Soroka's underlying numbers backed up his results, which makes him the more reliable choice.

Erick Fedde also caught my eye, but his 3.83 ERA sat well below his 5.01 FIP, the luckiest ERA of any pitcher I evaluated. That makes him a strong risk for a regression, so I elected to pass on pursuing him.

I also evaluated Zac Gallen. Milwaukee has an outstanding track record of developing pitchers, and he would have paired well with Soroka. However, the crowdsource does not list him, ESPN projects him at $12 million a year, and his 5.34 FIP was the worst of any pitcher I evaluated, so he stays on the list only as a buy-low option.

Brandon Woodruff could also return on a one-year deal. The crowdsource prices him at $16 million, and FanGraphs projects him for 2.0 WAR, which makes him a strong value on paper. However, the shoulder injury that kept him out after July 4 makes him too risky to count on.

The internal options shaped the rest of the plan. Misiorowski, Henderson, Gasser and Harrison give Milwaukee four starters, with Shane Drohan and Chad Patrick competing for the fifth spot. Drohan threw 124.2 innings with a 3.73 FIP in 2026, and Patrick posted a 3.40 ERA over 106 innings. However, FanGraphs projects both Drohan and Patrick to take a step back in 2027, so relying on this group alone is a risk.

Bullpen: With Romero and Senzatela gone, and the Brewers potentially trading Megill and moving Ashby or Uribe into the closing role, Milwaukee needs a reliable reliever. If the Brewers are looking for a left-handed reliever, Sean Newcomb is the best fit. Ashby and Hall return, but Zerpa and Fitzpatrick are recovering from Tommy John surgery. Newcomb threw 90.1 innings in 2026 with a 3.09 ERA, 3.21 FIP and 1.18 WHIP, can cover multiple innings, and projects for 0.8 WAR, tied with Gregory Soto for the highest of any left-handed reliever on the market, at $1 million less.

However, the bigger need is on the right side. Woodruff, Senzatela, May and Bryse Wilson are all departing, which leaves Milwaukee with just nine right-handed pitchers among the 18 on its 40-man roster. That group includes Quinn Priester, who missed all of 2026 with thoracic outlet syndrome and had surgery in July, and Megill, who may be traded. With Bednar, Pagán, Phillips and Loáisiga already signed, Kevin Ginkel makes the most sense. He made 74 appearances for Arizona in 2026 with a 4.12 ERA, 3.50 FIP and 1.12 WHIP, struck out 26.2% of hitters, and projects for 0.6 WAR.

The rest of the roster: For backup catcher, I wanted to get younger, so Bo Naylor takes the role rather than spending on a veteran. The Brewers also just acquired Ryan Walker, who covers Senzatela's innings in the bullpen.

That led to a plan built around three signings, with one more if ownership allows it.

Question 3

Who do we sign?

The plan adds one starter, one right-handed reliever and a first base platoon partner for $21.5 million, leaving payroll about $1.1 million under budget. If ownership is willing to spend about $33.5 million this offseason, Ty France is the upgrade at first base.

Michael Soroka is the starter to sign. He threw 116 innings for the Diamondbacks in 2026 with a 3.41 ERA, 3.19 FIP and 2.7 WAR, better than Peterson across the board, and his FIP backs up his ERA, so his results were not luck-driven. FanGraphs projects him for 1.8 WAR in 2027, worth about $15.5 million, and the crowdsource prices him at $18 million a year over two years. That is about $2.5 million more than his projection is worth. He also projects for 0.2 fewer WAR than Peterson, per FanGraphs. However, with his 2026 numbers being much better than Peterson's, I selected Soroka as the first option.

Kevin Ginkel adds the right-handed depth the staff is missing. He threw 67.2 innings for Arizona in 2026 with a 26.2% strikeout rate and a 6.2% walk rate, and his 3.50 FIP was well below his 4.12 ERA, another buy-low scenario. FanGraphs projects him for 0.6 WAR, worth about $4.4 million. He is not listed in the crowdsource, so I estimated $3.5 million based on his $2.725 million salary in 2026, about $0.9 million of surplus. He also picked up saves for Arizona in 2026, so he can help cover the late innings if Milwaukee trades Megill.

The other three moves add nothing to payroll. To get younger at backup catcher, Bo Naylor takes over Gary Sánchez's role. Ryan Walker is already on the roster and covers Senzatela's innings. At first base, LaMonte Wade Jr. on a minor-league deal gives Andrew Vaughn a left-handed platoon partner, with Blake Burke developing behind them.

David Peterson is the backup plan if Soroka signs elsewhere. His 4.79 ERA was misleading; his 3.96 FIP was nearly a full run better, which fits the profile Milwaukee has targeted successfully. FanGraphs actually projects him slightly higher than Soroka for 2027, at 2.0 WAR, worth about $23.5 million, and the crowdsource prices him at $11 million a year over two years, $12.5 million of surplus. Signing him in Soroka's place would save $7 million and leave payroll about $8.1 million under budget.

As covered above, he posted a 141 wRC+ and 3.6 WAR in 2026 but projects for 1.4 WAR in 2027, and ESPN noted his season may have been helped by luck. At $12 million a year over two years, almost exactly what his projection is worth, he is a fair-value signing rather than a bargain. He would be the everyday first baseman, with Vaughn starting at first base or DH against left-handed pitching and Wade no longer needed. The Padres are reportedly the most likely team to bring him back, and signing him adds about 1.3 more projected wins.

With France, the Brewers project for about 48.2 WAR in 2027, nearly matching the 48.7 they produced in 2026. Without him, they project for about 46.9. FanGraphs projects the current roster for 44.4 WAR, and the totals below add the new players to that:

France's at-bats would come partly out of Vaughn's playing time, so the real difference between the two plans is probably a little under 1.3 wins.

Trevor Megill is one player I would keep rather than trade. The Brewers are reportedly listening to offers for their closer, who is entering his final year of arbitration with a projected $8 million salary. However, Megill was the second most valuable pitcher on the staff in 2026, behind only Misiorowski, posting a 2.63 ERA, 1.85 FIP and 2.5 WAR over 61.2 innings. With Romero and Senzatela already gone, trading him would open another hole in a bullpen this plan is trying to fill. Keeping Megill alongside Uribe gives Milwaukee two proven late-inning arms, and his salary is already part of the arbitration estimate in the payroll above, so keeping him does not change the budget. If the Brewers do decide to trade him, Ginkel gives them a right-handed arm with late-inning experience to help cover those innings.

The core plan adds 2.5 projected wins for $21.5 million against the 7.8 lost, and adding France raises that to 3.8 for $33.5 million. Naylor is not counted because he joined the team in August 2026.

Question 5

Do they fit what we're trying to do?

Every move is consistent with how Milwaukee builds its roster.

  • Buy low on pitching. Soroka's 3.19 FIP was 0.22 better than his 3.41 ERA, and Ginkel's 3.50 FIP was 0.62 better than his 4.12 ERA. The Brewers have built their success on acquiring pitchers like these at a discount and improving them.
  • Payroll discipline. The core plan keeps payroll around $141 million, $1.1 million under budget, and adding France would take new spending to $33.5 million.
  • Balance. Soroka takes over May's right-handed rotation spot, Ginkel adds the right-handed relief depth the staff is short on, and Wade gives the right-handed Vaughn a left-handed platoon partner.
  • Completed before Dec. 1. None of these are headline signings that take all winter. All of them can be completed before a lockout freezes the market.

Shane McClanahan is a left-hander with a 3.50 FIP at about $6.6 million. With only one year of control remaining, the acquisition cost should be reasonable. His forearm would require a medical review, but acquiring arms below market value is the Brewers' formula.

Conclusion

The Brewers should sign Michael Soroka, Kevin Ginkel and LaMonte Wade Jr. for $21.5 million, adding 2.5 projected wins and leaving payroll $1.1 million under budget, with David Peterson as the backup plan. If they would rather add a left-handed reliever, Sean Newcomb is the alternative at $7 million. Naylor takes over at backup catcher, Walker covers the bullpen innings, and Megill stays at the back of the bullpen, with Ginkel as insurance if he is traded. If ownership allows about $33.5 million in spending, Ty France becomes the everyday first baseman and raises the total to 3.8 projected wins.

Neither version of the plan recovers every lost win; no plan at this budget could. The rest relies on what Milwaukee does better than almost any organization, which is getting more out of its players than the projections expect.

However, timing matters. If the league locks out the players on Dec. 1, no transactions can be made until a new agreement is reached. None of these moves require a long negotiation, and the Brewers can complete all of them now.
